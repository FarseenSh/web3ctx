The abstention count rides with the number, always — 20 / 147 in both arms. Precision computed only over answered questions would rise every time the server declined to answer, which would reward silence.

Not comparable to a published code-retrieval precision. CodeGrep and its kin retrieve within one repository: project selection never enters their metric, so under our definition their BM25 would read 1.000 — including the configuration measured at 0.375. The two numbers measure different stages, and ours was pre-registered as an upper bound where a pass is “suggestive, never proof”.

Composition: measured live against the deployed surface, macro-averaged, wildcard-version, both arms on the same questions. The two rows differ by one shipped mechanism and by nothing else — the identical abstention counts are the evidence for that.

The run's own banner, in its own words: directionally sound, NOT publication-grade. MCP payloads could not be byte-copied out of the reviewer's context, so the cached files are faithful transcriptions, not captures; and the token columns are asymmetric — ours self-reported and content-only, theirs measured on full payloads. Ours is the understated side.

The prediction was written before the run and stands either way, including the clause it got wrong: “you lose on breadth, you're competitive-to-better on tokens once truncation is fixed, and you win outright on version-correctness and on anything involving an address.” The token clause is false — measured, we were the most expensive of the four arms. Breadth was never tested by a designed experiment, and the run says so.

We lost T6 outright and both general-purpose tools caught it. The gap was content, not retrieval: the recipe did not state the rounding matrix. It does now.

n=5 questions, one reviewer, one afternoon. A competitive claim measured once is a claim about one afternoon — which is why the harness that makes this reproducible is the next piece of work, not a footnote.
